`src/topology_change/` — Breakup and coalescence

Public header: include/mftl_topology_change.h · Layer: 3 · Module group: Breakup and coalescence

A thread of liquid thinner than \(\delta_b\) breaks; a film between two surfaces thinner than \(\delta_c\) ruptures. Both are decided by the same detection, and both are carried out by the same reconstruction: rasterise the affected bodies' signed distance field on a lattice, bias it at every event, and triangulate its zero level set with marching cubes. Nothing migrates. Every step runs on the partition the caller has, and the result does not depend on the process count.

The pipeline

DETECT opposing triangle pairs within delta, within and between bodies
AGREE gather, canonicalise: an identical list on every rank
CLUSTER union-find over bodies joined by a two-body event
RASTERISE owned triangles into a window per cluster; exchange and merge overlap nodes
BIAS a bump at every event
EXTRACT marching cubes on own cells plus one ghost layer
LABEL components by their smallest triangle key, until nothing changes
BUILD decide ids, build objects, dense ids by holder scans
REDISTRIBUTE to the canonical partition
REMESH optional; then the size floor a second time
VOLUME three Newton steps per cluster
Figure 1. The reconstruction, run in 2D on a pinching dumbbell. (a) The neck is thinner than \(\delta_b\), and its opposing triangles are each behind the other. (b) The signed distance is rasterised only within \(r_\mathrm{cut}\); beyond it, the bulb centres included, nodes stay unknown. (c) A bump of height \(\varepsilon_b\) around the event lifts the neck above zero (dashed: the input surface). (d) The face rule extracts two closed loops. Every vertex lies on a lattice edge and is named by it, which is why neighbouring ranks need no stitching.

MFTLEnsembleApplyTopologyChanges is MFTLTopologyDetectEvents followed by MFTLEnsembleReconstructBodies on every body an event touches. A call that detects nothing changes nothing: no redistribution, no remesh, no pool touched. MFTLEnsembleReconstructBodies is public on its own because it is where every distributed property lives, and because re-gridding a body with no event at all is the cleanest end-to-end test of the pipeline there is.

Detection

Two triangles \(A\), \(B\) — of one body or of two — are an EVENT when

  1. they share no vertex;
  2. their unit normals oppose, \(\mathbf n_A\cdot\mathbf n_B \le -\cos\theta\);

  3. each lies on the same side of the other, along its own normal:

    \[ s_A = \mathbf n_A\cdot(\mathbf c_B-\mathbf c_A), \qquad s_B = \mathbf n_B\cdot(\mathbf c_A-\mathbf c_B) \]

    both negative — each is BEHIND the other, the gap is inside the liquid, a thread or a sheet: breakup. Both positive — the gap is outside, a film: coalescence. Mixed signs are a fold seen edge-on and are not events;

  4. their distance is within the threshold of THAT kind.

Condition 3 is what lets one predicate serve both directions. A Rayleigh–Taylor cap rolling onto its own stem and a jet's neck thinning are both "two opposing triangles within \_form#116 on one body"; only the side the gap is on tells them apart, so "intra-body means breakup, inter-body means coalescence" is wrong in both directions — a film inside one body coalesces, and a liquid film between a drop and a bubble inside it is a breakup between two bodies.

Figure 2. An event is classified by the side its gap is on, not by how many bodies it touches. Opposing triangles each behind the other enclose liquid: a breakup. Each in front of the other, they enclose a film of the other phase: a coalescence. So (c) a cap rolled onto its own stem coalesces within one body, and (d) the liquid film between a drop and the bubble inside it breaks between two bodies.

The pairs come from MFTLEnsembleIntersectObjects with self_pairs set — the existing distributed contact pipeline, with its halo, its broad phase and its ownership rule (a pair is reported by the owner of its A-side triangle) — and the opposing-normal test runs as a pair_filter BEFORE the narrow phase, since MFTL_TriTriProximity costs 176 ns per pair (`src/bvh/` — LBVH and contact detection) against one dot product, and most candidate pairs on a curved surface are neighbours facing the same way. The A-side tree is built with a zero envelope (the B side carries the whole \(\max\delta\)), which took the candidates from 129 to 45 per triangle at the same hit count.

The list is canonical. One neck is found once per triangle pair around it, and a pair across a rank boundary in general by both owners. Every event point is snapped to a cell of \(\tfrac14\max(\min\delta,\Delta x)\) and events are merged on (bodies, kind, cell), keeping the smallest gap — locally, and again over the gathered list. The result is a pure function of the geometry: identical on every rank and at every process count. The \(\Delta x\) in that quantum is load-bearing: on the threshold alone one jet neck was shredded into 779 events.

Clusters are the connected components of the listed bodies under the events between two of them — of EITHER kind. The two-body breakup case is the drop with a bubble inside it: the liquid film between them is bounded by both, and only a field over both surfaces sees the film.

The lattice

\[ \texttt{cells}_k = \texttt{cuts}_k\cdot\max\!\Big(1,\ \operatorname{round}\frac{L_k}{\texttt{cuts}_k\,\Delta x}\Big), \qquad \Delta x_k = \frac{L_k}{\texttt{cells}_k}, \]

anchored at global_min. Two properties follow and everything rests on them:

  • a node plane lies on every domain wall, so a surface that meets a wall is cut exactly there — an open surface's rim is reconstructed ON its wall, at lattice resolution, and carries MFTL_FLAG_BOUNDARY from the lattice key of its edge;
  • a node plane lies on every rank's cut plane, so every lattice cell belongs to exactly one rank, and "who triangulates this cell" has one answer everywhere.

The lattice is GLOBAL rather than one grid per cluster because of the second property: a per-cluster grid anchored at the cluster's box puts cell faces at arbitrary offsets from the cut planes, so the question "which rank extracts this cell" needs either a negotiation or an overlap. Memory is not the price it looks like — a rank allocates only a WINDOW (MFTLSdfGridInitWindow): its own box of the cluster's extent plus the cutoff, indexed modulo the period on a periodic axis. A rank whose box does not meet a cluster allocates nothing for it.

The signed distance

For a node \(\mathbf x\) and a triangle \(T\), MFTLClosestPointOnTriangle returns the closest point \(\mathbf p\) and the FEATURE it lies on — face interior, one of three edges, one of three corners — using Ericson's region test; a degenerate triangle is reduced to its nearest edge, and a corner result is bit-exact. The sign is that of \((\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf n_\text{pseudo}\), with the pseudo-normal of the feature:

feature \(\mathbf n_\text{pseudo}\)
face \(\mathbf n_T\)
edge \(\mathbf n_T + \mathbf n_\text{twin}\)
corner the ANGLE-weighted vertex normal

This is Bærentzen & Aanæs' result: with angle weighting the sign is exact for any closed, consistently oriented mesh, where area weighting is wrong at a spiky vertex — on randomly spiked spheres, of about 15 000 query points whose nearest feature is a vertex, angle weighting signs none wrongly and area weighting 122 (test_vertex_signs_on_a_spiky_mesh_need_angle_weighting, which keeps the area run as a control that must fail). Angle weighting is a second mode of MFTLComputeVertexNormals, not a second function, and MFTLUpdateGhostVertexNormals makes it exact on ghosts. The edge pseudo-normal needs the twin, which is why rasterisation reads the half-edge records rather than tris.neighbors alone.

A cluster is rasterised as a UNION. The field is the signed distance to the union of all its bodies' surfaces, signed by the nearest one — not the minimum of per-body fields, which is wrong for nested bodies: inside a drop that contains a bubble, \(\min(\phi_\text{drop},\phi_\text{bubble})\) is negative inside the bubble.

Figure 3. Why a cluster is rasterised as a union. A 1D cut through a drop containing a bubble, whose surface faces into the bubble. The distance to the union of both surfaces, signed by the nearest one, is negative exactly in the liquid. The minimum of the per-body fields, each known within \(r_\mathrm{cut}\) of its own surface, is negative inside the bubble, and next to the drop as well.

The merge is commutative and associative, so the field is partition-independent to the bit. MFTLSdfMerge keeps the smaller \(|\phi|\) and breaks a tie towards NEGATIVE. Each rank rasterises only the triangles it OWNS — each triangle exactly once in the job — into its window, and sends every node another rank needs; the merged value is the same whatever order the contributions arrive in, and equal to a serial rasterisation of the whole mesh. The alternative, pulling every neighbour's nearby triangles and rasterising them locally, ships a triangle near a corner to seven ranks and rasterises it on eight; pushing moves one double per boundary node.

There is no flood fill. A node farther than \(r_\text{cut}\) from every triangle stays MFTL_SDF_UNKNOWN, and with

\[ r_\text{cut} = \big\|(\Delta x_0,\Delta x_1,\Delta x_2)\big\| + \max(\varepsilon_b,\varepsilon_c) + \tfrac12\Delta x \]

a cell with an unknown corner provably cannot be crossed by the biased zero level set, so marching cubes skips it without needing to know which side it is on. A flood fill would be a global, sequential sign propagation across ranks — exactly what the cutoff removes.

The bias

Rasterising and re-extracting a thread of radius \(0.4\,\Delta x\) reproduces it or loses it depending on where the lattice nodes happen to fall. The event, not the lattice, should decide, so every event adds a smooth bump to the field around its point before extraction:

\[ \phi \leftarrow \phi + \varepsilon\, w\!\left(\frac{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_e|}{\rho}\right), \qquad w(r) = \begin{cases} 1 & r \le \tfrac12 \\ \operatorname{smoothstep}(1 \to 0) & \tfrac12 < r < 1 \\ 0 & r \ge 1 \end{cases} \]

with \(\varepsilon = +\varepsilon_b\) for a breakup (the thread's inside becomes outside) and \(-\varepsilon_c\) for a coalescence (the film becomes inside). Overlapping bumps combine by the maximum per sign, not by summing, so ten events on one neck bias it once. \(\beta > 1\) decides a feature just at the threshold with margin. \(\rho\) is the trade the header documents with measurements: full weight out to \(\tfrac12\rho = 0.75\,\delta\) by default reaches past the surface of any thread thinner than \(\delta\), and a wider bump spends the bias on the bodies either side (2.9 % against 4.0 % volume per bulb at \(\gamma = 1.5\) against 2 on the pinched dumbbell, before the correction below puts it back).

Marching cubes

The 256-case table is generated from one rule applied to each face of the cell, depending only on that face's four corner signs, so two cells sharing a face draw the same segments on it and the surface cannot crack between cells; loops are fanned from an apex none of whose diagonals lies in a face, so no triangle lies in a cell face. src/mesh_creation/marching_cubes.c has the full argument; test_marching_cubes.c enumerates all 256 cases for closure, orientation and the absence of in-face triangles.

A vertex is named by the lattice edge it lies on, and a triangle by 16 * cell + slot. Those keys are what makes the distributed extraction need no stitching: each rank extracts its own cells plus one layer of its neighbours', so the triangles around every vertex it owns are present — the vertex-complete halo, built by the extraction — and two ranks that both produce a vertex produce it from the same two node values and name it the same.

Positions are computed from WRAPPED node indices. On a periodic axis the same lattice edge is reached as node \(n\) by one rank and as node \(n - N\) by the rank across the seam; computing the position from the unwrapped index gives two positions one period apart that the owner test then sends to two different owners. Measured on the travelling-wave example at np = 2: holes and \(\chi = 15\) for an annulus. Guarded by test_seam_vertices_get_one_owner_on_every_rank, which fails when the wrap is removed.

Bodies from triangle soup

A component's label is the smallest triangle key it contains — a pure function of the component, so every holder converges on the same number without electing anything. Each round: every LOCAL component takes the smallest label of its triangles (MFTLLabelConnectedComponents did the local connectivity), every holder of a ghost triangle tells its owner the label it has, and the owner lowers its component to the smallest it hears and answers. A component crossing \(k\) rank boundaries needs at most \(k+1\) rounds, and the loop ends on a global "nothing changed". Objects are built per GLOBAL label, not per local component: a U whose bend is on the next rank is two local components and one body.

Who keeps which id is decided identically on every rank from one Allgatherv of (cluster, label, owned triangles, open) records — one per component and holder, a number sized by the change, not by the ensemble. Per cluster, components largest first take the ids of the bodies that went in, largest first; components beyond that get fresh ids; ids beyond that are retired. So the jet a drop pinches off stays the jet, and the pool a drop falls into stays the pool. A CLOSED component below min_body_triangles is deleted (an open one never is).

Vertex and triangle ids are made dense with MFTLEnsembleExscanBodies — each owner numbers what it owns from its holder-scan base — and a ghost asks its owner for the id of the entity with its key. The closing MFTLRedistribute then rebuilds the canonical halo from correct ids. MFTLObjectSetCounts marks every new object partition-dirty, so the redistribution's body skip cannot mistake one for settled.

Rims

A level set has no boundary except where it meets the lattice's edge, so an open surface is representable exactly when its rims lie on domain walls — which, on the lattice above, is where node planes are. The reconstruction therefore reproduces a rim on its wall and flags it from the lattice key; a cluster containing a rim anywhere else is REFUSED (n_clusters_refused) and left untouched rather than corrupted. Detected by one flag per cluster on a reduction already being taken.

After the extraction

The closing MFTLRedistribute restores the canonical partition, and when opts.remesh is set one MFTLRemeshEnsemble runs over the whole ensemble.

The size floor is applied twice. A satellite a couple of lattice cells across comes out of marching cubes as a few dozen slivers and clears min_body_triangles; remeshed at a target edge length near its diameter it becomes a tetrahedron, which the collapse approval refuses to dissolve and which then survives as four triangles each facing against all three neighbours. That is exactly what the Rayleigh–Taylor example reported at np = 4 as "4 inverted triangles". So the count is taken again after the remesh, from one sum per component of THIS reconstruction. test_a_satellite_the_remesher_collapses_is_discarded fails without the second count.

The volume goes back LAST. Marching cubes cuts chords across a curved surface, the bias moves liquid, and the remesh that cleans the slivers up smooths some away on its own; on the liquid jet, without the correction, 60 of a final 81 volume units were lost over 45 reconstructions. Per cluster, the enclosed volume is measured before and after (a closed body directly; an open one together with the walls its rims are on, as the flux of \((x_k - x_{k,\min})\,\mathbf e_k\) along an axis whose far wall carries no rim), and restored by a uniform offset \(d\) along a displacement field \(\mathbf u\): the area-weighted vertex normals, with a rim vertex's wall-normal component removed. The step is Newton's, on the exact first variation of the volume,

\[ \frac{dV}{dd} = \sum_t \mathbf S_t\cdot\frac{\mathbf u_0+\mathbf u_1+\mathbf u_2}{3}, \]

( \(\mathbf S_t\) the area vector), which each rank sums over the triangle corners it owns — every (triangle, corner) pair is present once in the job because the halo is vertex-complete. Dividing by the area instead converges only linearly, at about 0.03 per step. Measured from a 15 % deficit: \(2.8\cdot10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-9}\) after two and three Newton steps, against \(1.3\cdot10^{-4}\), \(3\cdot10^{-6}\), \(8\cdot10^{-8}\) after two, three, four area steps. The count is FIXED at three: a stopping test on a reduced double would be two ranks disagreeing in the last ulp about whether to enter another collective.

Each choice below was either made before implementation or forced by a measurement.

What is tested, and how

tests/test_topology_change.c runs every scene twice — distributed, and on rank 0 alone with MPI_COMM_SELF — and compares a CENSUS of the result: per body its vertex, triangle and boundary counts, Euler characteristic and volume, and the sorted positions of every vertex, quantised so that a last-bit difference in a volume-correction sum cannot reorder a tie. At np = 1, 2, 4, 8 and 27 the distributed surface equals the single-rank surface to \(10^{-12}\). The scenes are placed where the cut planes of every tested grid meet, on the periodic seam, and across walls; each also checks the partition invariants and dense ids. The unit pieces are tested where they live: test_geometry.c (closest point, every feature region, degenerate triangles), test_signed_distance.c (the merge laws, sign against the winding number on hard shapes, rasterising in pieces bit-identical to all at once), test_marching_cubes.c (all 256 cases), test_bvh_self.c (self-proximity as a SET against an exhaustive sweep, at np up to 27, with a cross-rank non-vacuity count), test_topology.c (component labelling) and test_vertex_normals.c (angle weighting).

The three open-surface examples carry it into flows, each with a non-vacuity flag that fails a run in which the body count or genus never changed: test_liquid_jet_topology (a drop pinches off and travels to the outlet), test_rayleigh_taylor_topology (a blob pinches off the rolled-up cap at t ≈ 3.5) and test_travelling_waves_topology (a plunging lip reconnects with the face).

Costs, and what is not done

  • A reconstructed body is re-triangulated EVERYWHERE, not only at the event: vertices, ids and pool order are new, and the surface moves by the marching cubes error, \(O(\Delta x^2\kappa)\). Bodies outside every cluster are not touched at all. A local reconstruction that re-extracts only a neighbourhood of each event and stitches it to the old mesh would avoid that; it needs a seam between a lattice surface and a remeshed one, and was not attempted.
  • Detection runs every call and is the part that scales with the whole ensemble's surface rather than with the change: one self- and pair-proximity query over every body. It dominates a call that finds nothing, which is most calls.
  • There is no continuous-time detection. Two surfaces that approach by more than \(\delta\) in one step are not found, exactly as for contact detection (Open issues).
  • Rims off the walls are refused, not handled. A level set cannot represent them; a surface with a free rim needs a different reconstruction.
